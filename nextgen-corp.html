HR Analytics Case Study

HR Analytics & Workforce Insights

Client: NextGen Corp  |  Industry: Technology (Software & Hardware)  |  Tools: PostgreSQL, SQL

A SQL-driven HR analytics project uncovering employee retention risks, performance gaps, and compensation inconsistencies to support workforce planning at a rapidly scaling technology company.

1. Business Problem

NextGen Corp was scaling fast — but rapid growth had created blind spots in how the business managed its people. Turnover was happening, but HR could not pinpoint which departments were most at risk or why employees were leaving. Performance varied across teams, but without data-driven segmentation, interventions were broad and imprecise. Compensation had evolved organically over time, creating inconsistencies that were beginning to affect morale — without anyone fully understanding the extent of the problem.

The analysis was designed to answer four strategic HR questions:

  • Where are retention risks most concentrated, and what is driving them?
  • Which departments have the greatest performance gaps?
  • Are compensation levels aligned with performance across departments?
  • What HR interventions would have the greatest workforce impact?

2. Data Overview

Analysis was conducted on a relational HR database restored into PostgreSQL, comprising six interconnected tables:

  • Employees: Profile, hire date, department, salary, performance score, attendance rate
  • Departments: Structure, classifications, and reporting lines
  • Performance: Historical performance scores enabling trend analysis
  • Attendance: Presence and absence records by employee and period
  • Turnover: Employee exit records and reasons for leaving
  • Salaries: Salary history and adjustments over time

GDPR Note: All employee data was handled with strict confidentiality. Outputs were produced at department and role level — no individual employee profiles were included in reports. Salary and performance data was accessed under the assumption of internal HR use only, consistent with legitimate interest provisions under GDPR.

3. Data Cleaning & Preparation

What Was Removed

  • Duplicate employee records identified through Employee ID matching across tables
  • Incomplete turnover records where exit reason was not recorded

What Was Transformed

  • Hire dates converted to calculate employee tenure in years
  • Performance scores segmented into three tiers: High (≥5.0), Mid (3.5–4.9), Low (<3.5)
  • Salary data joined with performance data at department level for compensation alignment analysis

New Calculated Fields Created

  • Turnover Rate: Exits ÷ average headcount per department over the analysis period
  • Average Tenure: Mean employment duration in years by department
  • Salary-Performance Index: Average salary relative to average performance score by department, used to identify compensation misalignment

4. Data Analysis

Key KPIs Tracked

  • Turnover Rate by Department
  • Average Employee Tenure
  • Performance Score Distribution (High / Mid / Low)
  • Average Salary by Role and Department

Key Trends & Patterns

  • Turnover is not evenly distributed — specific departments show disproportionately high exit rates, suggesting company-wide retention initiatives may be misallocating resources.
  • High performers (score ≥5.0) exist across all departments, but their distribution is uneven — some teams concentrate high performers while others are dominated by low scorers.
  • Compensation analysis reveals departments where average salary is above benchmark relative to performance, and others where high performers are paid below the departmental average.
  • Attendance patterns correlate with performance tier — employees in the low performance segment show higher absence rates, a potential early warning indicator for future turnover.

Insights

  • Retention risk is department-specific. The concentration of high turnover in particular departments points to local management, team culture, or workload issues — targeted interventions will be more effective than broad policy changes.
  • Underpaid high performers are a flight risk. Employees delivering strong results but earning below the departmental average have both the ability and the motivation to seek better-compensated roles elsewhere.
  • Low performance combined with high absence is a predictive signal. This segment represents both a current productivity concern and a near-term turnover risk — early intervention is more cost-effective than managing exits reactively.
  • Compensation inconsistency is not always intentional, but it is always costly. Where salary and performance are misaligned, the business is either overpaying for underperformance or creating retention risk by underpaying contributors.

5. Recommendations

  • Conduct targeted retention reviews in the highest-turnover departments — specifically investigating exit reasons, management practices, and workload distribution.
  • Initiate a compensation equity review for high-performing employees earning below departmental average. Proactive salary adjustments are significantly less costly than recruiting and onboarding replacements.
  • Implement structured performance improvement plans for low-performing, high-absence employees — combining clear expectations with access to coaching or additional support.
  • Use performance and attendance data to build an early warning dashboard that flags at-risk employees before they reach the turnover stage.

6. Limitations

  • The dataset reflects a point-in-time snapshot — longitudinal analysis over multiple years would strengthen trend detection and predictive modelling.
  • Exit reason data was incomplete, limiting the depth of turnover cause analysis. A structured exit interview process would significantly improve data quality for future work.
  • Compensation analysis does not account for total remuneration (benefits, bonuses, equity) — base salary alone is an incomplete proxy for compensation competitiveness.
  • Performance scores may carry subjective bias from manager assessment. Calibration across departments would improve the reliability of cross-team comparisons.

Business Impact

NextGen Corp now has a clear workforce risk map built on evidence rather than assumption. Retention risks are visible by department and can be addressed before they result in costly exits. Performance gaps are quantified and linked to specific teams, enabling precise rather than broad interventions. Compensation misalignments have been identified and can be corrected in a way that protects both fairness and retention. For a company scaling rapidly, the ability to make data-driven people decisions is not a nice-to-have — it is a competitive necessity.
